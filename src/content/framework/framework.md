Every organisation that publishes has a way of doing content. Very few have a model of it. A content operating model is that model made real: a designed, written-down account of how your organisation decides, produces, maintains and retires content, from the beliefs the words rest on to the workflow that ships them on a Tuesday afternoon.

The distinction matters more than it used to, because a new kind of colleague has arrived who cannot learn the way you do things by watching. People absorb an organisation's habits over months of meetings. An AI agent gets what is written down, and nothing else. If your operating model exists only as behaviour, machines can't follow it; and increasingly, neither can anyone else.

## A way of doing content isn't an operating model

The way is easy to point at. A sign-off ritual adds four days to every page. New ideas get met with "we don't usually do that". One person knows why the pricing page is worded so carefully, and what happened in 2021 when it wasn't. None of this was designed; it accreted, decision by decision, until it hardened into how things get done.

You couldn't call that an operating model. It's chaotic and ad hoc and not really modelled at all. A model is a representation: something that exists outside the heads of the people running it, that a new starter could read, a leadership team could argue with and a team could improve on purpose. If the way your content gets made lives only as behaviour, what you have is habits.

Habits also age badly. They're why the review-everything-annually rule sits in a slide deck somewhere and has never once fired, and why 78 pages of health guidance can stay live while contradicting the policy that changed in the spring.

Which is why "what's our AI strategy?" is the wrong question in most of the rooms it gets asked in. The answer is an operating model. The machines don't remove the need to decide how content gets made; they remove the option of never writing the decisions down.
