## You are here

For too long content has been seen as the purview of comms and marketing. A production function looking after blog posts and social media.

Meanwhile, the things that determine the success or failure of organisations, the policy reports quoted by government committees, the support content that saves lives, the conference speeches, the instructions for what to do when there's a tsunami, happen in other places.

And a whole other sea of less glamorous but still business-critical words and pictures, internal emails, briefings, slide decks and job descriptions, sloshes around organisations taking little notice of the standards and governance that public-facing content goes through.

Ways of doing all these things do usually exist in our organisations, but they tend to be ad hoc, dictated as much by habit as systemic governance. Templates sit on personal hard drives, conventions live in people's heads, collaborative processes depend on personalities. And the rules that tell us how we put words together sit on page 99 of brand guidelines that are almost exclusively about visual identity.

AI helps with none of this. In fact it makes lots of it worse, amplifying different approaches, painting a layer of plausibility over a disparate collection of documents and emails, and turning up the quantity dial so that the floodwaters of slop rise exponentially.

AI didn't invent slop; it was already a rising tide that the web was drowning in. Nor did AI invent robotic content: the corporate world managed to produce plenty of that long before Claude came along.

But it makes problems we already had much bigger and much more pressing.

It doesn't have to be this way.

## Learning from past mistakes

When digital transformation came around, we mostly focused on the tech. We spent large amounts of money buying new websites, until they overflowed with content, at which point we spent even more. We spent even larger amounts of money on CRMs and hoped they’d solve our relationships with people outside the organisation. We employed editors who knew how to edit CSS and set up digital teams that were subsets of comms. We started with the tech and seldom stood back to see the bigger implications and opportunities.

Now we risk doing the same with AI. We obsess with models and chatbots. We all use it and then spend large amounts of time and effort to cover up the fact. We moan about em dashes and X not Y, and spend almost no time thinking about what Good looks like. We use it on an individual level, sometimes on a team level, and seldom on an organisational level. We tie ourselves in knots thinking about what not to do, and creating guardrail policies and too little time thinking positively about what we could do better.

This is our opportune moment to look at the bigger picture and to think, plan and build transformatively and constructively.

## AI is content, mostly

The vast majority of what AI is changing in organisations is content. Content in the widest sense: all the words, all the pictures, all the video, all the charts, all the illustrations. The things we make, the things we write and say. Internally and externally.

Almost all the things that make an organisation function. Most of the things that make an organisation succeed or fail.

Expertise in content-as-a-system exists but is usually pigeon-holed away from senior management teams, seen as an efficiency challenge for comms and marketing.

The reality is more transformational than tactical. AI is a massive challenge and a massive opportunity for organisations. To do more, but also to do better.

If we’re going to grab that opportunity we need to stop treating it as a tech-first issue and look at what it enables and what we need to transform in our organisations in order to get there.

## Devops as inspiration

We have much to learn from developers, who are the pioneers in human-and-AI-collaboration, and who already had a devops practice that was impressively mature. Despite our best efforts, generally more mature than content strategy.

The coding world has gone through an extraordinary, rapid transformation. From “AI can help autocomplete what I’m typing” to “I’m designing agentic systems with swarms of sub-agents.” The work hasn’t disappeared but it has certainly changed.

A scaling up can be seen in this world: there’s a continuous line from the micro to the macro. From prompt engineering through context engineering and graph engineering to agentic systems.

Outside of the world of development our organisations are still mostly operating at the micro level, thinking about prompting better and how Claude can help clear our inboxes faster.

## The operating model answer

AI can help us improve quality as well as speed. It can be a vital part of a collaborative system of humans and robots working together to a common purpose. It can scale up our impact and help organisations achieve things that were previously far out of reach.

But it can only do that if we build a holistic operating model that works for everyone across the organisation and that details how that collaborative work happens.

That operating model needs to include all of the important organisational context as well as the instructions and processes, the roles for humans and the roles for AI and the handoffs between them. The things that make you you, and the people you’re connected to, in all the ways, as well as the structures, the workflows and the strategies.

Clarity on these things was always useful, but it’s now vital. Because AI can’t read habits and cultural norms, and left to its own devices it will produce homogeneity, or, worse, uninformed nonsense and slop, filling in the gaps with what others think and say or what it made up.

Developers might call this the harness, the scaffolding. But for an organisation it needs to be more than that. It needs to be a complete operating model for both humans and AI. A Content Operating Model.
